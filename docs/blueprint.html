01 · Product requirements

A shield that asks for something back

Existing blockers gate the morning reflex with a timer or a friction tap. Daybreak gates it with the one thing a morning actually needs: a written decision about what the day is for.

Problem

The first thing most of us do with a phone is open whatever is loudest. Screen Time limits and third-party blockers interrupt that reflex, but they ask for nothing in return, so the interruption is skipped as fast as it appears. A limit that only measures minutes does not change what the day is spent on.

Daybreak turns the shield into a prompt. From a start time you choose, the apps you picked are shielded by iOS itself, not by an app that can be force-quit. The only way through is to write your goals for the day and a short reflection, and to have that writing pass a bar you set in advance. Tap Done reflecting and the apps are yours until tomorrow's start time.

Who it is for

You, on your own iPhone. The gate is self-imposed: there is no account, no partner, no parent, and nothing you write leaves the device. Daybreak never even learns which apps you picked; iOS hands it opaque tokens.

Goals and non-goals

Goals for v1

  • Enforced by the system. From the start time, the picked apps show a shield even if Daybreak is not running, was force-quit, or the phone rebooted.
  • One decision a day. Writing 3–5 goals and a reflection takes under five minutes, then the apps stay unlocked until tomorrow.
  • An honest bar. Approval is immediate, deterministic, and explains exactly what is missing.
  • Private by construction. No network, no server, no analytics; everything lives in the app's own container.
  • Keeps the record. Every approved reflection is kept as a journal entry you can reread.

Not in v1

  • Parental or family mode, or anything involving a second person.
  • Cross-device sync, cloud backup, or a web view of the journal.
  • AI-graded reflections. The bar is a local rubric; a Claude-powered coach is a phase 2 option, designed for behind the same protocol.
  • Time limits, usage stats, or streak gamification beyond a plain streak count.
  • An evening or end-of-day review.
  • Android, iPad layouts, widgets, or a watch app.

The bar: what "good enough" means

Approval is a rubric, not a judgement call. Every check is deterministic, runs on device in milliseconds, and produces a specific fix when it fails. The user picks a strictness once; the thresholds below are what each level enforces.

CheckRelaxedStandardStrictFix shown when it fails
Enough goals (max 5)2+3+3+"Add at least three goals."
Each goal is specific3+ words4+ words5+ words"Goal 2 is too short. Say what done looks like."
Goals start with an actionoffonon"Start goal 1 with what you'll do: Send…, Finish…, Call…"
One goal is measurable (a number, a time, or a deadline)offoffon"Give one goal a number or a time."
No repeated goalsononon"Goals 1 and 3 say the same thing."
Reflection length25+ words40+ words80+ words"12 more words. What matters most today, and why?"
Reads like real writing (varied words, real letters)ononon"This doesn't read like sentences yet."
Different from yesterday's goalsoffoffon"These match yesterday. What's new today?"

How "action" is detected. The goal's first word is compared against a list of common action verbs (finish, send, write, call, run, book, read, review…). If that misses, the NaturalLanguage framework's part-of-speech tagger looks for any verb in the line. Either signal passes. This keeps the check forgiving of phrasing while still rejecting "gym" or "the report" as a goal.

How "real writing" is detected. At least 40% of the reflection's words must be distinct, and the share of vowels among its letters must sit in the range that real prose occupies (roughly 20–65%). That rejects "asdf asdf asdf" and "blah blah blah" without needing a language model.

Non-functional requirements

Reliability

  • Shield state is held by iOS, so it survives force-quit, reboot, and low-power mode.
  • Every app launch reconciles: schedule registered, shield matches policy.
  • Extensions are tiny and stateless; they read a handful of defaults and return.

Privacy

  • Selection is stored as opaque system tokens; the app cannot enumerate app names.
  • No network entitlement is used at all.
  • Journal entries are plain JSON in the app group container, deleted with the app.

Performance

  • Rubric evaluation completes on every keystroke in well under a frame.
  • Shield clears within a second of Done reflecting.
  • No timers or background polling; the system wakes the monitor extension.

Accessibility

  • Dynamic Type throughout; no fixed-height text containers.
  • Every check in the rubric list is a labelled row for VoiceOver.
  • Colour never carries a state on its own: pass and fail also differ by icon and text.

Open questions

  • Weekends. Same start time every day in v1. A per-weekday time is a small Settings change later.
  • Escape hatch friction. Turning the schedule off currently asks for one confirmation. If the metric above shows it being used, add a delay (for example, takes effect tomorrow).
  • Websites. The picker allows web domains; v1 shields whatever is picked without a separate opinion about Safari.
  • AI coach. A phase 2 grader could judge whether goals are realistic and specific, at the cost of a network call and an API key on device. Kept behind the rubric protocol so it can be added without touching the UI.

02 · User flow

One setup, then the same loop every morning

The first-launch row runs once. The loop below it runs daily: iOS raises the shield, you write, the rubric approves, the shield drops until tomorrow.

04 · Tech stack & architecture

Four targets, one shared folder, no dependencies

iOS owns the schedule and the shield. Daybreak owns the decision. Three small extensions carry Daybreak's instructions into the parts of the system the app cannot reach while it is closed.

The four sequences that matter

A · Save the schedule

you → app → iOS
  1. User saves 7:00 AM (S3 or S9). App validates it is before 11:45 PM.
  2. App writes startHour, startMinute, scheduleEnabled = true to the app group.
  3. App calls startMonitoring(.daily, during: 07:00→23:59, repeats: true).
  4. App runs the policy for now: inside the window and not cleared → applies the shield itself (FR-5).

B · Interval starts

iOS → monitor extension
  1. At 7:00 AM iOS launches the monitor and calls intervalDidStart(.daily).
  2. Monitor reads scheduleEnabled, clearedDay, and selection.
  3. If enabled and clearedDay ≠ today: sets shield applications, categories, and web domains on the “daily” store.
  4. iOS now draws the shield over any picked app until the store is cleared.

C · Done reflecting

you → app → iOS
  1. Rubric verdict is approved; the button enables.
  2. App appends a ReflectionEntry to journal.json and deletes the draft.
  3. App writes clearedDay = today, then clears the “daily” store.
  4. Today shows the cleared phase. Tomorrow's intervalDidStart sees clearedDay ≠ today and shields again.

D · App comes to the foreground

app (every launch and return)
  1. Read authorization status; if not approved, show S1.
  2. If scheduleEnabled but the system no longer lists the activity, call startMonitoring again (FR-14).
  3. Compute the phase for now and reconcile: shielded → apply; cleared or before start → clear.
  4. Render Today from the phase. No timers: the view recomputes on foreground and on a one-minute clock while visible.

Concurrency and isolation

  • The app target is main-actor by default. Views, AppModel, and the services are ordinary synchronous code on the main actor. The only await in the app is the authorization request.
  • The extensions are nonisolated by default. iOS calls intervalDidStart and the shield callbacks on its own threads; the overrides must match the superclass's isolation, so those targets do not opt into main-actor default.
  • Shared types are explicitly nonisolated so they mean the same thing in both kinds of target. They are value types with no shared state; the only reference type they touch is UserDefaults, which is thread-safe.
  • No background work. The rubric runs in microseconds, the journal is a few kilobytes, and the schedule is the system's problem. There is nothing to move off the main thread.

Setting up the build

  1. Generate the project and open it: 
    xcodegen generate
open Daybreak.xcodeproj
  2. Pick your paid developer team under Signing & Capabilities for all four targets, or set DEVELOPMENT_TEAM once in project.yml and regenerate.
  3. With automatic signing, Xcode registers the Family Controls (development) capability and the app group on each App ID. If it refuses, add both at developer.apple.com under Identifiers, then retry.
  4. Run on a physical iPhone on iOS 17 or later and accept the Screen Time prompt. The Simulator runs the whole UI through the mock gateway but cannot shield anything.
  5. First device test: set the start time two minutes ahead, background the app, wait, open a picked app and expect S7. Open Daybreak, reflect, tap Done reflecting, and expect the app to open. Reboot and expect it to stay open. Next morning, expect the shield back.
  6. Before TestFlight or the App Store, request the Family Controls distribution entitlement from Apple. Development builds do not need it; distribution builds will not sign without it.

Testing strategy

Automated, in the Simulator

  • Rubric: every check at every strictness, parameterized over passing and failing drafts, including gibberish and duplicate goals.
  • Policy: the four phases across the start time, the day boundary, a cleared yesterday, and the schedule being off.
  • Day keys and streaks across month ends and time-zone changes.
  • One UI test walks the first morning end to end on the mock gateway: onboarding, an empty form rejected with its fix, goals and reflection typed, the day unlocked, the entry in the Journal.

Manual, on a device

  • The six-step script above, plus: change the start time while shielded, change apps while shielded, turn the schedule off and on.
  • Revoke Screen Time access in Settings and confirm the app recovers to S1.
  • Watch the monitor extension in Console.app filtered by the app's subsystem.

Phase 2 candidates

  • Claude-graded reflections. A second ReflectionApprover that sends the draft to Claude with the rubric as instructions and returns the same verdict shape. Needs an API key in the Keychain and a network entitlement; the local rubric stays as the offline fallback.
  • Evening check-in. A second, optional interval that asks which goals were met, feeding a weekly view in the Journal.
  • Per-weekday start times and a "not on weekends" switch.
  • Lock Screen widget showing the phase and the countdown, and an App Intent so "Start reflecting" works from Siri and Shortcuts.
